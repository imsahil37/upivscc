\chapter{Literature Review}

This chapter looks at past research to help us understand the shift away from plastic cards. It covers both the psychological theories behind why people adopt new technology and real world data that shows exactly how this change is happening.

\section{Theoretical Frameworks}

Moving from physical cards to digital payments is not just a tech upgrade. It is a big change in human behaviour tied to psychology and social pressure. To make sense of this, my study uses two famous theoretical models.

\subsection{Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)}

Fred Davis created the Technology Acceptance Model in 1989. It is one of the most popular ways to explain how people accept new technology. The model says that two main things make a person want to use something new. These are \textbf{Perceived Usefulness (PU)} and \textbf{Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU)}.

\subsubsection{Perceived Usefulness (PU) in Financial Technology}

PU basically means how much a person thinks a new system will make their life or job better. When we talk about consumer payments, this means making transactions faster and getting more value out of them.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Managing Cash Flow:} The biggest benefit of Credit on UPI is that it gives regular people access to credit for everyday things. Before this, you could not use a credit card to buy vegetables or pay the local barber.
    \item \textbf{Rewards on Small Spends:} Indian shoppers love getting a good deal. Old credit cards gave reward points, but you usually had to shop at big stores to get them. Credit on UPI lets you earn rewards on almost every single scan.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU) and the ``Scan'' Paradigm}

PEOU is just how easy and effortless someone thinks a system will be to use.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Less Hassle:} Paying with a physical card takes a few annoying steps. You have to find your wallet, pull out the card, put it in the machine, and type a PIN. Just scanning a QR code feels way faster and smoother.
    \item \textbf{Going Wallet Free:} Having credit right inside the UPI app means you do not even need to carry a wallet anymore. The credit card is now just software sitting on your phone.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT)}

In 2003, Venkatesh and his team combined several older theories to create the UTAUT model. This model points out four main things that drive people to use new tech. These are \textbf{Performance Expectancy}, \textbf{Effort Expectancy}, \textbf{Social Influence}, and \textbf{Facilitating Conditions}.

\subsubsection{Performance Expectancy (PE): Speed and Economic Utility}

PE is basically whether people think the system will actually help them get things done faster or better.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Instant Money:} For shop owners, a good system means getting paid quickly. UPI uses real time transfers, so merchants get their money right away instead of waiting days.
    \item \textbf{High Success Rates:} UPI rarely fails or crashes, which makes people trust that it will perform well when they need it.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Social Influence (SI): The Network Effect of QR Codes}

SI is all about peer pressure. It measures how much you are influenced by seeing your friends and family use a new system.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Everyday Language:} In India, phrases like Google Pay it or Paytm it have become regular verbs. Seeing QR codes on every shop counter makes you feel like you have to use them to fit in.
    \item \textbf{Younger Users:} The data shows that \textbf{45\%} of people using credit on UPI are under 30. Young people tend to copy what their peers are doing, which drives adoption fast.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Facilitating Conditions (FC): Smartphone Penetration and DPI}

FC looks at whether the right tools and systems are in place to make using the technology possible in the first place.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Public Tech Infrastructure:} The government's push for banking, Aadhaar, and mobile connectivity ev erywhere laid the perfect groundwork for this to succeed.
    \item \textbf{Government Rules:} The RBI officially stepping in to allow RuPay credit cards on UPI was the final green light the system needed.
    \item \textbf{Phones Everywhere:} With over 600 million smartphones in India today, almost everyone already has the hardware they need to use this tech.
\end{itemize}

\section{Empirical Studies}

\subsection{Global Parallels: The ``Pix'' Revolution in Brazil}

The closest thing to India's UPI story is the \textbf{Pix} system in Brazil. Their central bank launched it in late 2020.

\subsubsection{Duarte and Team (2022): The Substitution Effect}

In a major paper about instant payments replacing cash, researchers looked at data from 2020 to 2022. They found that people heavily swapped cash for Pix. Even more interesting, people slowly stopped using their debit cards too.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Main Discovery:} For small purchases under BRL 50 (around \rupee 800), Pix usage shot up while debit card swipes stayed flat.
    \item \textbf{Why This Matters for India:} This looks exactly like the Indian market for tiny daily purchases. But the study also showed that people still used credit cards for big, expensive items. This proves that pay later options survive better than simple debit cards.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Chinese Paradigm: Alipay vs. UnionPay}

China is a great example of a market that has already matured. QR codes like Alipay and WeChat Pay are used by almost everyone. This has created massive competition for their traditional state backed card network called UnionPay.

\subsubsection{Chen and Zhang (2021): Platform Ecosystems}

These researchers pointed out that QR payments succeeded because they were built into super apps. Users did not just open the app to pay. They used it to chat, book cabs, and order food all in one place.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Takeaway:} Physical plastic cards are basic tools that only send payment numbers. Mobile apps are smart and keep users engaged. This gives apps a huge advantage over regular plastic.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Domestic Literature: The Indian Context}

\subsubsection{NITI Aayog (2023): Digital Payments Report}

A report by NITI Aayog pointed out a very important gap between the number of transactions and the actual money spent.

\begin{quote}
    \textit{``While UPI accounts for 75\% of retail digital transaction volumes, credit cards still command a disproportionate share of transaction value.''}
\end{quote}

This quote perfectly supports my study's main idea. UPI wins when it comes to frequency, like buying milk every day. However, physical cards win on value, like buying a new fridge. Putting RuPay credit cards on UPI is an attempt to get the best of both worlds.

\subsubsection{RBI Bulletin (April 2024): ``The Merchant Perspective''}

A survey by the RBI looked at small merchants in smaller towns. It found that \textbf{88\%} of them preferred UPI simply because there were no hidden transaction fees (MDR). The study concluded that these extra costs are the biggest reason small businesses refuse to use plastic cards.

\subsubsection{Bhattacharya (2023): Credit on UPI and Financial Inclusion}

In an article for the \textit{Economic and Political Weekly}, Bhattacharya looked at how Credit on UPI could help poorer people get access to loans. The study found that issuing virtual credit through UPI apps is very much cheaper for banks. It cuts down the cost of getting new customers by up to 70\% compared to mailing out physical cards.

\subsubsection{PwC India (2024): The Indian Payments Handbook}

A recent report by PwC predicted that by 2029, Credit on UPI could take over 15\% to 20\% of the entire credit card market. They believe it will mostly eat into medium sized purchases ranging from \rupee 2,000 to \rupee 10,000.
