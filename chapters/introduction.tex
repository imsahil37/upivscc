\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background of the Study}

\subsection{Evolution of Money: From Barter to Plastic}

The history of money is actually just about making it easier for people to buy and sell things. Over time, we have moved from trading physical goods to just sending digital data. Basically, we have always tried to separate the ``value'' of money from its physical form. To understand why physical credit cards are now losing out to virtual QR codes, we first need to look at how we went from carrying heavy coins to swiping plastic.

\subsubsection{The Pre-Monetary Friction and the Double Coincidence of Wants}

In the beginning, people used the barter system. But this was super inefficient because both people had to want exactly what the other person was trading. This made doing business really slow. Then came commodity money, like trading salt or cows. This helped give things a set value, but it was hard to carry around or keep fresh. Eventually, standardizing metal coins (gold, silver, and copper) fixed the freshness problem, but they were still heavy and risky to transport.

The first big change happened when paper money was introduced in China and later Europe. This was huge because the paper itself had no real value. It only had value because the government promised it did. Still, even with paper money, you had to be there in person to hand it over right when you bought something.

\subsubsection{The ``First Supper'': The Birth of the Diners Club}

The big jump from paying right now with cash to paying later on credit happened in the mid-1900s. There is a famous story called the ``First Supper.'' In 1949, a businessman named Frank McNamara was eating at a restaurant in New York and realized he had forgotten his wallet. This embarrassing moment gave him an idea. He wondered if there was a single card that let trustworthy people eat out and pay the bill later without needing cash.

He teamed up with his lawyer and started the \textbf{Diners Club} in 1950. The first card was not the shiny plastic we know today. It was actually made of cardboard and meant mostly for travel and food expenses. By 1951, tens of thousands of people were using it, and by 1953, it was accepted internationally in places like the UK, Canada, and Mexico. This kicked off the ``Plastic Era,'' even though the cards were not quite plastic yet.

\subsubsection{The Material Transition: Cardboard, Celluloid, and the Dominance of PVC}

People quickly realized that cardboard cards were ruined too easily. In the late 1950s, American Express stepped in with the first cards made of \textbf{celluloid}. This was better than cardboard, but it eventually became brittle and cracked over time.

The real game changer came in the 1960s with \textbf{PVC} plastic. PVC was flexible, tough, and could hold the magnetic stripes (and later, smart chips) that make modern credit cards work. During the 60s and 70s, as companies like Visa and Mastercard grew, the plastic PVC card became the global standard for payments. For over 60 years, ``plastic'' meant ``credit.'' But today, as everything moves online, the physical limits of plastic are starting to hold us back.

\subsection{The Dominance of the Card Networks: The Four-Party Model}

To really understand why ``Credit on UPI'' is such a big disruption, we have to look at the old system it is trying to replace, which is the Four-Party Model. This setup was perfected by giants like Visa and Mastercard and has run global digital payments for fifty years. It works well globally, but it is really complicated and expensive. That is why it never fully took over in price-sensitive places like India.

\subsubsection{Deconstructing the Legacy Ecosystem}

Even though it is called the Four-Party Model, there are actually five main players involved in making a single swipe work. This strict setup ensures a card from London works seamlessly in Mumbai.

\begin{description}[style=nextline]
    \item[1. The Consumer:] The person buying things with their plastic card.
    \item[2. The Merchant:] The shop selling the goods, who signs up to accept card payments.
    \item[3. The Acquirer (Merchant's Bank):] The bank that gives the shop the card swipe machine (POS) and makes sure they actually receive their money.
    \item[4. The Issuer (Consumer's Bank):] The bank that gives the consumer the card, sets their credit limit, and takes on the risk.
    \item[5. The Network (Visa/Mastercard/RuPay):] The middleman that connects the two banks, sets the rules, and securely routes the transaction data.
\end{description}

\subsubsection{The Authorization-Clearing-Settlement Cycle}

Every time you swipe a card, it starts a fast digital relay race:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Authorization:} You swipe the card, and the data goes from the shop to the merchant's bank to the network and finally to your bank. Your bank checks if you have enough credit. If yes, it sends an approval back down the line to the swipe machine.
    \item \textbf{Clearing:} At the end of the day, the shop bundles all its transactions. Their bank sends this bundle to the network, which figures out exactly which bank owes money to whom.
    \item \textbf{Settlement:} The actual cash is finally moved between the banks, and the shop's bank deposits the money into the shop's account.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{The Economics of Intermediation: Merchant Discount Rate (MDR)}

The biggest problem with this setup, especially in developing countries, is the \textbf{Merchant Discount Rate (MDR)}. This is the fee the shop has to pay just to accept a card, usually between 1\% and 3\% of the sale amount.

For small Indian shops (like local Kirana stores) that only make a tiny 5\% to 10\% profit margin, giving up 2\% to fees just does not make sense. On top of that, buying the swipe machine used to cost around \rupee 8,000 to \rupee 12,000, plus monthly rent and internet fees. Because it was so expensive, only big stores and rich city areas accepted cards. Small businesses were forced to stick to cash.

\subsection{The Indian Digital Payment Revolution: The Genesis of UPI}

While the West kept tweaking the plastic card system, India created something completely new. On April 11, 2016, India launched the \textbf{Unified Payments Interface (UPI)}. Created by the NPCI and regulated by the RBI, UPI was not just built as a private app to make money. It was built as a public system for anyone to plug into.

\subsubsection{The Architecture of Disruption: Identity as Address}

Credit cards rely on a 16-digit number hard-coded onto a piece of plastic. UPI is totally different because it uses a simple virtual ID, almost like an email address (like name@bank). It runs on existing fast-payment technology, meaning the money actually moves between bank accounts instantly, 24/7. This is a huge upgrade from the old card networks, which usually take a day or two to fully settle the money.

\subsubsection{Quantitative Analysis of Growth (2016 to 2025)}

The way UPI has grown is honestly unprecedented. It basically shot straight up and totally changed how digital payments work on a massive scale.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Start (2016 to 2018):} Growth was steady at first, mostly pushed by demonetization and early tech adopters.
    \item \textbf{The COVID-19 Boost (2020 to 2022):} The pandemic forced people to avoid touching cash. This turned UPI from a cool convenience into an absolute necessity.
    \item \textbf{Taking Over (2023 to 2025):} By the 2023 to 2024 financial year, UPI was handling about \textbf{79.7\%} of all digital payments in India.
    \item \textbf{Where It Is Now (2025):} Today, the system processes a massive \textbf{18 to 20 billion transactions} every single month.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Global Benchmarking: UPI vs. Visa/Mastercard}

UPI is not just a big deal in India anymore; it is globally dominant. With around 640 million transactions a day, UPI easily matches or beats Visa's daily numbers worldwide. This represents a monumental shift. A free, home-grown public system in India is now processing as much volume as the world's biggest credit card network, but it costs a fraction of the price and is available to almost everyone.
