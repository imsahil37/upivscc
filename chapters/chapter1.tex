\chapter{Research Topic}

\section{Introduction and Personal Context}

When I first started thinking about a topic for my final year dissertation, I just looked around me. I noticed something very basic but fascinating. My father's generation still carries a thick leather wallet everywhere. It has everything---cash, old receipts, a debit card, and usually one or two premium credit cards. But when I look at my friends in college, or even young professionals taking the metro with me in Delhi, the story is completely different. Most of us just carry our smartphones. Our phone is our wallet, our bank, and our identity. We barely carry cash, maybe just a \rupee 50 note hidden behind the phone cover for emergencies.

The evolution of money is usually taught in textbooks as a straight line: barter system to coins, then to paper money, and finally to plastic cards. For a very long time, the ultimate symbol of financial independence in India was getting your first physical credit card. That shiny piece of plastic meant you had arrived. You could walk into a store, buy a TV or a laptop, swipe the card, and say, "I'll pay for this later."

But in the last few years, things have taken a crazy turn in our country. The Unified Payments Interface (UPI) came out of nowhere and completely flipped our daily habits. At first, UPI was just a quick way to split a restaurant bill with friends or pay the local auto-rickshaw bhaiya directly from our bank accounts. It was convenient, but it was just using our own money. It wasn't credit. People who needed to manage their monthly budget, or wanted to buy expensive things on EMIs, still heavily relied on swiping their traditional credit cards.

Then, in 2022, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) made a massive announcement. They allowed RuPay credit cards to be linked directly to UPI apps like Google Pay, PhonePe, and Paytm. Honestly, this felt like magic to a lot of users. Suddenly, you didn't need to carry that piece of plastic. You could go to a neighborhood shop, scan the same old, slightly faded QR code sticker on the wall, and pay using your credit card limit. The money wouldn't leave your bank account right away; you'd get a bill at the end of the month, just like a regular credit card.

This dissertation dives deep into this massive change. I wanted to find out if this new "Credit on UPI" trend is really going to kill the traditional physical credit card. Are we looking at the end of the plastic era? Or is there a catch that we aren't seeing yet?

\section{Looking Back: The Cash-Heavy Indian Mindset}

To really understand why this shift is such a big deal, we have to look back at how India functioned barely a decade ago. We have always been a deeply cash-centric society. "Cash is King" wasn't just a saying; it was how households ran.

I remember my mother keeping cash hidden in different dabbas (boxes) in the kitchen for different expenses---one for the milkman, one for the newspaper guy, and one for emergencies. Giving credit, or "udhaar," was also highly informal. You had a good relationship with the local Kirana store uncle, and he would just write down your purchases in a small diary. At the beginning of the next month, when salaries arrived, you would go and clear the cash dues.

Formal credit was very rare and honestly, a bit scary for the average middle-class family. Applying for a credit card involved filling out endless forms, providing income tax returns, salary slips, and waiting weeks for approval. Even if you got a card, using it felt risky. There were constantly stories about card cloning, hidden charges, and falling into massive debt traps. Furthermore, where would you even use it? In the early 2010s, only big showrooms in malls or fancy restaurants had those heavy Point of Sale (POS) swipe machines. The local vegetable vendor or the cobbler on the street had never even seen one.

Because of this lack of infrastructure, physical credit cards became a luxury product. They were meant for buying flight tickets or booking hotels, not for everyday life.

\section{The Turning Points: Demonetization and COVID-19}

Two major shocks forced India to change its habits. The first was the demonetization of \rupee 500 and \rupee 1000 notes in November 2016. Overnight, the cash in everyone's pockets became useless paper. I distinctly remember the chaos. Suddenly, people were forced to download digital wallets like Paytm just to buy basic groceries. It was a steep learning curve. Older people struggled with smartphones, and shopkeepers were hesitant to trust money they couldn't physically touch. But necessity is a great teacher.

The second shock was the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. The fear of catching the virus from touching physical cash accelerated digital adoption like nothing else. "Contactless delivery" became the buzzword. People who had previously resisted learning how to use UPI had to figure it out to survive lockdowns.

By the time the pandemic restrictions lifted, the habit was set. The National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) had created a masterpiece with UPI. It wasn't a closed app where you had to load money; it was a public network connecting almost every bank in the country. It was completely free for both the buyer and the seller. Because it was free, shopkeepers everywhere---from the fanciest boutique in Connaught Place to the smallest pan-wallah in a narrow alley---put up a QR code.

\section{The Mechanics: How the Old System Actually Works}

Before jumping into why Credit on UPI is disrupting things, it's worth understanding why the old credit card system is so clunky and expensive. It runs on something called the "Four-Party Model," which is heavily controlled by American giants like Visa and Mastercard.

When you swipe a physical credit card at a store, it's not a simple transaction. It involves a whole relay race of data.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The Consumer:} You, holding the card.
    \item \textbf{The Merchant:} The shopkeeper who wants to get paid.
    \item \textbf{The Acquirer Bank:} The bank that gave the shopkeeper the swipe machine.
    \item \textbf{The Issuer Bank:} The bank that gave you the credit card (like HDFC or SBI).
    \item \textbf{The Network:} Visa or Mastercard acting as the bridge.
\end{enumerate}

The moment you swipe, data travels from the machine to the acquirer, then to the network, then to your bank to check if you have the credit limit. If yes, the approval signal travels all the way back. It happens in seconds, which is amazing, but it costs money.

The shopkeeper has to pay rent for the swipe machine, pay for the internet connection it uses, and most importantly, pay a fee called the Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on every single swipe. This MDR is usually around 1.5\% to 3\% of the sale amount. If a shopkeeper sells a shirt for \rupee 1,000, they might only get \rupee 980 in their bank account. The remaining \rupee 20 is split between the banks and the network.

For big businesses with high profit margins, giving up 2\% is fine. It's the cost of doing business. But for a local grocery store owner who only makes a 5\% profit margin on a packet of biscuits, giving up 2\% to the bank is practically unbearable. That is the fundamental reason why swipe machines never reached the millions of small shops in India.

\section{The UPI Credit Revolution}

This brings us to the core topic of this research. What happens when you combine the massive spending power of a credit card with the cheap, universal infrastructure of UPI QR codes?

The RBI and NPCI saw an opportunity. Since millions of QR codes were already printed and pasted everywhere, why not let people use them for credit? They introduced RuPay credit cards that could be linked to UPI apps.

Think about what this means in daily life. Suppose it's the 25th of the month, and a student's pocket money is running low, or a young professional is waiting for their salary on the 1st. They need to buy some medicines or groceries. Instead of struggling with cash, they can just go to the local pharmacy, scan the QR code with their GPay, select their RuPay Credit Card as the payment source instead of their savings account, and walk out. The transaction takes 3 seconds. The shopkeeper gets paid instantly, and the user pays the bank next month. No swipe machine required. No carrying a wallet.

It sounds perfect on paper. But as I started digging into the topic and talking to people for this project, I realized the ground reality is a lot messier.

\section{Deep Dive: The Psychology of Credit vs. Debit}

One of the most fascinating aspects I uncovered while setting up this research was the psychological difference between spending your own money (debit/savings) and spending the bank's money (credit). This is a well-documented phenomenon in behavioral economics known as the "pain of paying."

When you hand over physical cash, your brain registers a loss. You physically see the money leaving your hand. This triggers a mild psychological pain, which naturally regulates your spending. When you swipe a debit card, that pain is slightly reduced because you don't see the cash, but you still know your bank balance is instantly decreasing.

Credit cards completely detach the pain of paying from the act of consuming. You get the product today, but you don't feel the "loss" of the money until the bill arrives 30 to 45 days later. This delay often leads to higher spending. Retailers know this, which is why supermarkets encourage credit card usage.

Now, bring UPI into the equation. UPI has already gamified the payment experience. Scanning a code and hearing the little "beep" or the voice confirmation box saying "Received" feels almost like scoring points in a video game. It is incredibly frictionless. When you attach a credit line to this completely frictionless interface, the psychological barrier to spending drops to near zero.

A significant part of my research motivation was to observe if this hyper-convenience is actually a trap for younger consumers. Are we scanning our way into debt? This behavioral shift forms a massive undercurrent to the entire dissertation.

\section{Case Study: The Sarojini Nagar Observation}

To contextualize the problem, I spent a Saturday afternoon in Sarojini Nagar market in South Delhi. It is one of the busiest, most chaotic, and unorganized apparel markets in the country. It is the perfect microcosm of the Indian retail sector.

Ten years ago, Sarojini Nagar was 100\% cash. Today, every single vendor---from the massive garment shops to the guy selling socks on a plastic sheet on the pavement---has a UPI QR code hung around his neck or taped to a pole.

I observed around 50 transactions at various stalls. In 48 out of 50 transactions, the customer used a smartphone to scan a QR code. Only two people used cash. \textit{Zero} people used a physical credit or debit card. There were no POS machines visible anywhere except in the large, air-conditioned showrooms at the edge of the market.

This observation solidified my premise: the physical infrastructure battle is already over. The traditional card network has lost the street. If banks want to lend money to the millions of people shopping in places like Sarojini Nagar, they absolutely must route that credit through the existing QR codes. The physical plastic card is functionally useless in this environment.

\section{Statement of the Problem}

The transition from swiping plastic to scanning QR codes for credit is facing some serious hurdles. The problem I am trying to investigate is not just technological; it is highly behavioral and economic.

The main problems are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Merchant Rebellion:} Shopkeepers got used to standard UPI being totally free. Now, when a customer uses Credit on UPI, the bank charges the shopkeeper that old MDR fee (around 1.5\% to 2\%). Shopkeepers are getting frustrated. Why should they pay a fee for a QR code scan when it was free yesterday? Many are starting to fight back, putting up signs saying they won't accept credit transactions via UPI.
    \item \textbf{The Reward Points Addiction:} People who use premium credit cards are obsessed with rewards---cashback, air miles, free lounge access at airports. The physical cards offer great rewards because they charge high fees to the merchants. If UPI credit has capped or lower fees, the banks can't afford to give out those lavish rewards. Will a consumer really give up their free flight tickets just for the convenience of scanning a QR code?
    \item \textbf{The Habit Factor:} We are creatures of habit. Swiping a card for a big purchase feels secure. Tapping a phone screen for a \rupee 50,000 laptop purchase somehow feels risky or anti-climactic to older generations. I wanted to see if age plays a big role in this shift.
\end{itemize}

\section{Why Does This Study Matter?}

I chose this topic because it is happening right in front of our eyes. It is not some old historical event; the rules of the game are changing every month as the RBI releases new guidelines.

Firstly, it matters for us, the consumers. If Credit on UPI takes over completely, we might see the end of heavy wallets. Our entire financial identity will be tied to a smartphone. Understanding this shift helps us see where personal finance is heading for our generation.

Secondly, it matters deeply for the small business owners. I spoke to many local vendors during my data collection, and you can see their genuine frustration. They feel squeezed between demanding customers who only want to scan, and banks that secretly cut fees from their hard-earned money. If this issue isn't solved, the whole system might collapse.

Finally, it is a multi-billion rupee headache for the banking sector. Banks have spent decades building their physical card businesses. They have huge marketing teams pushing Visa and Mastercard products. Now, a homegrown Indian system is threatening to make all that plastic irrelevant. Banks have to figure out how to survive and make a profit in a world where the customer never actually touches the card they are issued.

\section{Objectives of the Study}

While researching over the past few months, I set a few clear objectives to keep my work focused:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item To compare the frequency of usage between traditional physical credit cards and Credit on UPI among different age groups in Delhi NCR.
    \item To understand what exactly makes a consumer choose to swipe a card versus scanning a QR code (analyzing the ticket-size factor).
    \item To investigate the ground-level reaction of small and medium merchants towards the MDR fees charged on UPI credit transactions.
    \item To predict whether physical credit cards will become obsolete or if they will evolve into a niche luxury product.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Boundaries of the Research}

Since the topic of digital payments is incredibly vast, I had to set some boundaries for this dissertation so I wouldn't get overwhelmed.

\textbf{Geographical Scope:} I kept my primary data collection strictly to the Delhi National Capital Region (NCR). Delhi NCR is a great testing ground because it has everything---from ultra-luxurious malls in South Delhi where people swipe premium cards without thinking, to crowded unorganized markets like Sarojini Nagar and local street food vendors where QR codes rule.

\textbf{Demographic Scope:} I actively tried to get responses from different generations. I categorized them roughly into Gen Z (the college kids and fresh graduates), Millennials (the working professionals who have families), and Gen X (our parents' generation). It was important to see how different age groups trust technology.

\textbf{Time Frame:} The secondary data I looked at covers the period from roughly 2020 (the COVID-19 push) to early 2025. The digital space changes so fast that looking at data from 2015 wouldn't really help answer today's questions.

\subsection{A Quick Note on Limitations}
No research is perfect, especially when done by a student with limited time and resources. Because I only surveyed people in Delhi NCR, the findings might be a bit skewed towards urban, tech-savvy behavior. A farmer in a rural village or a shopkeeper in a tier-3 town might have completely different opinions about credit and smartphones. Also, people aren't always completely honest on surveys. They might say they spend carefully, but their actual bank statements might show a different story. I had to rely on their self-reported answers. Lastly, the RBI keeps changing rules regarding MDR caps and RuPay limits, so what is true today might shift slightly by next year.
